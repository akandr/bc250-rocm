# What the persistent journal shows that dmesg could not, 2026-08-20

Generated by `scripts/journal_retro_sweep.sh`.

## Why this exists

The soak crash of 20 August established that checking `dmesg` after a board
reset reports the boot that followed the crash, so it cannot see the fault that
caused it. Every "no faults in dmesg" statement in this work that covers a run
ending in a reset was therefore reading an empty buffer.

The board keeps a persistent journal, 491 MB covering twenty boots back to
17 August, so those runs can be re-examined rather than merely doubted. That is
what this directory is: the same question asked of an instrument that could
actually have answered it.

## Result

Six of the twenty retained boots contain GPU faults, preemption failures or
resets. `boot_sweep.txt` holds the full listing.

| boot | kernel | configuration | what the kernel logged |
|---|---|---|---|
| -16 | 7.1.5 | `sched_policy=2` | 12 cp queue preemption timeouts, no reset |
| -13 | 7.1.5 | recommended | page fault, preemption failure, 4 runlist flush failures, reset, SIGBUS |
| -8 | 7.1.5 | recommended | 10 runlist flush failures, reset |
| -3 | 7.1.8 | recommended | page fault, reset |
| -2 | 7.1.8 | recommended | page fault, preemption failure, 3 runlist flush failures, reset |
| -1 | 7.1.8 | recommended | page fault, preemption failure, runlist flush failure, reset, SIGBUS |

Boot -16 is not a surprise and not a defect report: that boot deliberately
carried `amdgpu.sched_policy=2`, which this work already documents as wedging
sustained compute, and the timeouts are the documented behaviour of the arm.

The other five are the finding. All five ran the configuration this repository
recommends, across both kernels it has measured, and every one of them ended:
the boot's last journal entry comes 2 to 4 seconds after `GPU reset begin` in
every case. The reset is not survived. Only one of the five logged a
reset-succeeded line, and the board went down regardless.

Two of those statements cannot be checked from what is shipped, noted 26 August.
`boot_sweep.txt` records per-boot event counts by category and carries no
timestamps and no reset-succeeded tally, so the two-to-four-second gap and the
one-of-five figure come from reading the journal directly. That journal has since
rotated, as this page says of the rate below, so neither can be recovered. The
six-boot table above is fully backed by the sweep, event for event; the two
sentences here are not.

## What this changes

The write-up carried this as a rare event, first as a host-side SIGBUS "seen
once in about twelve runs", then, after the soak reproduced it with a full
kernel trail, as one occurrence in 254 rounds. Both figures were artifacts of
the instrument. Against the journal the count is five fatal resets in twenty
retained boots, two of them carrying the SIGBUS signature. The rate per unit of
work cannot be recovered, since the retained boots are not a uniform sample of
campaign activity and some were short or idle, so the honest statement is a
count over boots rather than a frequency.

The direction of the correction is what matters: the defect is more common than
this work has been saying, and it was undercounted for a mechanical reason
rather than a statistical one.

## Caveat on timestamps

Wall-clock times are not comparable between these boots. The board's clock
jumps across reboots, so `journalctl --list-boots` reports several boots whose
last entry precedes their first. Boots were identified here by kernel release
and command line rather than by time, and no attempt is made to map them to
particular experiments.

## This count cannot be re-derived from the board

The figure is a snapshot of what the journal held on 20 August, and
`boot_sweep.txt` is the record of it. Running the same sweep later gives a
different and smaller answer: by 21 August it reported four boots with a reset
out of eighteen retained, because the journal rotates and the oldest of the six
boots listed above have aged out.

Two deliberate GPU resets were triggered on 21 August while testing whether a
reset is survivable at all. They do not inflate any later count, because they
killed the board before the kernel logged anything, so they leave no trace in
the journal at all. The effect of running this sweep now is therefore to
undercount rather than to overcount, and the number above should be read as
what was observed in that window rather than as a rate anyone can reproduce on
demand.
